1. How CESSB works

Controlled Envelope Single Sideband is Dave Hershberger's (W9GR) method for raising the average power of an SSB voice signal without raising its peak envelope power (PEP). A transmitter's power amplifier is limited by its peaks. If the peaks are controlled, the average can rise, and the signal sounds louder at the same PEP, by 3–5 dB in practice. The processing is entirely digital and happens on the complex baseband I/Q signal before the modulator.

Why SSB voice has high peaks

Voice already has a high crest factor. Generating SSB adds more. The Hilbert transform that makes the quadrature channel shifts every harmonic of the voice by 90°, and the harmonics then line up differently, producing envelope peaks that the audio waveform never had. Hershberger measured these SSB peaks at 61 % above the audio's peak. On the W9GR voice clip, Phoenix's own transmit chain produces I/Q with a peak-to-average ratio of 8.7 dB. A transmitter set for a given PEP spends most of its time far below it.

Clipping the audio before the Hilbert transform doesn't solve this, because the peaks are created after it. Clipping I and Q separately after it does limit them, but it is a square clip: the envelope can still reach √2 of the limit, the phase is distorted, and the signal splatters. That is what Phoenix did before CESSB when the microphone was overdriven (see the "today, overdriven" trace in Figure 2).

The three stages

Let z = I + jQ be the baseband SSB signal, scaled so the allowed envelope is 1.0.

  1. Envelope clipper. Wherever |z| > 1, set z ← z / |z|. This is a radial clip. The magnitude is limited and the phase is untouched, so the envelope never exceeds 1.
  2. Lowpass filter. Clipping spreads the spectrum, so a linear-phase FIR removes everything outside the SSB channel. Filtering a clipped signal brings back some overshoot, about 1 dB of peaks.
  3. Overshoot controller. The filtered signal is clipped again, but only the difference is kept: d = z − z / max(|z|, 1), with the envelope maximum taken over a few neighbouring samples. This difference is filtered with the same lowpass and then subtracted, scaled by gainCompensate, from the signal delayed to line up with it. Filtering is linear, so subtracting a filtered correction is the same as filtering a corrected signal: the result stays in the channel. Setting gainCompensate > 1 over-corrects, to make up for the way filtering smears the correction.

The result, in Hershberger's figures, is overshoot reduced from 61 % to 1.3 %. W7PUA reports 3–4 dB more average power at about 2:1 drive into the clipper. Anything after CESSB must be linear-phase (FIR, not IIR), or the relative phases change and the peaks return.

2. The zero-IF problem, and centring

The envelope |z| does not depend on where the signal sits in frequency: |z·ejωn| = |z|. The clipping can therefore happen at any frequency offset. The filtering cannot.

  • Hershberger's original, and W7PUA's Weaver class. Weaver modulation centres the SSB channel on DC, about ±1.35 kHz. A real lowpass on I and Q then removes clip products on both edges of the channel.
  • Zero-IF transmitters, such as Phoenix and W7PUA's Z class, put the channel at 0…3 kHz on one side of the carrier. A real lowpass on I and Q has a symmetric response: it passes −3…+3 kHz. Clip products that land in the opposite sideband pass straight through. W7PUA measured about −20 dB there at 4 dB of enhancement. Phoenix's model of the Z class gives −27 dBc on voice, and −11 dBc for the two-tone product 500 Hz into the opposite sideband.
  • Weaver output on zero-IF hardware leaves a 1350 Hz tone wherever the mixers leak local oscillator. This is why W7PUA wrote the Z class.
Phoenix uses the centred form. The zero-IF signal is shifted down by the channel centre (1.5 kHz), clipped and filtered with a ±1.45 kHz lowpass, and then shifted back. The clipping decisions are identical, because the shift does not change the envelope. The filters now act as complex bandpasses centred on the channel, so they cut both of its edges. The output is still zero-IF, so there is no new tone. The cost is two complex multiplies per sample and about 0.3 dB of talk power.

3. The Phoenix implementation

Where it sits

Phoenix already generates SSB by decimating the microphone signal to 12 kHz, applying a ±45° Hilbert pair, and interpolating back. CESSB is inserted as a native stage at the existing Fs/8 node: 24 kHz at 192 ksps, 22.05 kHz at 176.4 ksps. That is the point where the signal is complex SSB at a comfortable rate.

5. Testing

Four layers, each tied to the one below: an offline model, unit tests on the real firmware code, a bench suite on the radio's exciter, and an RF suite at the antenna connector.

5.1 Offline model (code/tools/cessb_model)

A Python model of TransmitProcessing() built from the firmware's own coefficients. The fixed tables are parsed out of DSP_FIR.cpp, and the generated stages come from a line-for-line port of CalcFIRCoeffs. Its −3 dB transmit corner matches the firmware's unit test to about 1 Hz at both rates. The model chose the variant and its parameters, and predicts the bench results (12 self-tests).

5.3 Bench suite (code/tools/cessb_hil)

The AD2's W1 output drives the microphone input, and its Ch1/Ch2 inputs capture the exciter's I and Q together. CAT switches CESSB off and on at every point, so every result is an A/B on the same signal. Every stimulus is periodic and fits the AD2's waveform buffer exactly, so each run, and each radio, gets an identical signal, and the model can predict the result for that same signal:

  • 1 kHz tone, stepped in level: finds the ceiling.
  • Two-tone, 700 + 1900 Hz: products on known frequencies.
  • Steady vowel "ah": a Rosenberg glottal pulse train through three formant resonators.
  • Syllable "ah": the vowel in a 0.15 s raised-cosine burst every 0.25 s. This is the voice stand-in: in the model it gains 2.4 / 4.1 / 5.2 dB at 3 / 6 / 9 dB of drive, against 2.8 / 4.6 / 5.3 dB for real speech.

Final run on the KI3P prototype: 176.4 ksps, 5 W into a dummy load, 103 s of transmission, 45/45 checks pass. Repeat runs agree within 0.01 dB on the ceiling and 0.1 dB on gain.

5.4 RF suite (code/tools/cessb_hil/cessb_rf_test.py)

The same AD2 stimuli, measured at the transmitter's output: after the RF board, the PA and the low-pass filters. The radio feeds a dummy load, and a 60 dB tap goes to a Rohde & Schwarz CMU200. Its narrow-band power meter, with a 1 MHz filter, reports the average and the largest instantaneous power, which is PEP. That was checked first: a steady tone reads max − average = 0.23 dB, and a linear two-tone 3.04–3.16 dB (theory 3.01).

The suite never overdrives the PA with CESSB off. CESSB off is measured only below the ceiling: at the transparency points, and as a linear reference just under it.

Final run on the KI3P prototype: 14.175 MHz USB, 5 W, 176.4 ksps, 5.5 minutes of transmission in short keyed sections. 25/25 checks pass.

Spectrum. At RF the PA, not CESSB, sets the spectrum:

  • The PA's own distortion. With CESSB off, a linear two-tone already has 2f₁−f₂ at −29.9 dBc (−36 dB re PEP) and out-of-channel power at −48 dB re PEP.
  • Two-tone with CESSB on at 6 dB: 2f₁−f₂ at −30.8 dBc and opposite sideband at −32.9 dBc, no worse than linear. Out-of-channel energy is 2.2 dB higher, which fits the PA's own products: its PEP is 1.2 dB higher than the reference's, and third-order products rise about 2 dB per dB.
  • Voice with CESSB on: out-of-channel power is −72.9 dB re PEP at 6 dB drive (linear voice: −71.5) and −59.9 dB at 9 dB.
    • Why it rises at 9 dB: the signal is denser, at 4.7 times the average power for the same PEP, so the PA's intermodulation rises with it.
    • Even then it is 12 dB below the transmitter's own linear two-tone.
    • A ±25 kHz max-hold sweep shows nothing above the analyzer floor (about −77 dB re PEP) beyond ±4 kHz.
  • Voice opposite sideband is −38.7 dBc with CESSB at 6 dB, the same as linear. That is the radio's image floor.

5.5 What the tests found

  • Peak regrowth in the analog exciter is small. The codec and analog exciter add about 1.5–2 % of peak over the model's figure. The worst case on the bench is 3.5 % (0.3 dB).
  • The 2f₁−f₂ product is 7 dB worse at 176.4 ksps than at 192 ksps (−55.8 against −62.7 dBc), in both the firmware and the model. It is still far below the Z class's −11 dBc. The unit test now checks both rates.
  • The bench cannot resolve CESSB's opposite sideband. On the KI3P prototype, the exciter's own image of a tone moves between about −40 and −63 dBc from one capture to the next, at every frequency, with CESSB on or off. A one-sample I/Q timing skew was ruled out. This is a property of the radio, now logged for investigation. The bench's opposite-sideband check is therefore only a gross-failure guard (−25 dBc).
  • The AD2 is not accurate at a few millivolts. It moves ±1 dB between captures at 5 mV. The suite ignores levels below 20 mV for the transparency check.
  • No peak regrowth through the PA. At RF, peaks with CESSB on land at or just below the ceiling. The PA compresses the brief peaks slightly: a two-tone's PEP lands 0.5–0.8 dB under the steady tone's.
  • The KI3P prototype's RF chain compresses about 1 dB over the top 7 dB below full power. Each 1.5 dB step of a tone gives +1.3 to +1.4 dB at RF, against +1.53 dB at the exciter. Its two-tone IMD3 is −36 dB re PEP at 5 W on 20 m.
  • A faulty cable between the main board and the RF board showed up on the first RF check. Both sidebands came out at the same level: double sideband. After the repair, a 1 kHz tone has its opposite sideband at −48.5 dBc and the carrier at −34 dBc.
  • The first firmware build filtered the voice away (centre frequency on the wrong side), and the filter was half a sample off-centre. Both were caught by unit tests before the radio was keyed.

6. Using CESSB on Phoenix

What changes when CESSB is on

  • The SSB power setting becomes a peak limit. Voice peaks are held to the power you set: measured at the antenna, 5.06 W PEP for a 5 W setting, however hard the voice is driven. Without CESSB, loud peaks could exceed the setting.
  • Average power rises as more of the voice is pushed up to that limit. On an average-reading wattmeter, the needle swings higher as you raise the level. PEP does not. Measured on the voice stand-in at 5 W PEP: 0.23 W average unprocessed, 0.51 W at level 3, 0.79 W at level 6 and 1.08 W at level 9.
  • Only SSB voice is affected. Digital (USB audio) modes, CW, tune and all the calibration routines bypass it automatically.
  • About 5 ms of latency is added to transmit audio.

What the level setting means

The ceiling is the envelope that produces exactly your SSB power setting. The CESSB level is extra gain, in dB, applied to the voice just before it reaches the ceiling, on top of the microphone gain.

  • Level 0 dB: the voice reaches the clipper as the mic gain delivers it. If your peaks only just reach the ceiling, CESSB acts as a clean peak limiter and processing is minimal.
  • Level L dB: your peaks are pushed up to L dB past the ceiling, and CESSB removes those L dB from the peaks without splatter. The quieter parts of speech rise by up to L dB, so the average goes up.

Measured gain, on the voice stand-in, when the voice peaks reach the ceiling at level 0:

Each dB of processor level has the same effect as a dB of mic gain once the voice is at the ceiling, so either knob moves the C bar. Use the mic gain to set the voice level and the CESSB level to set the amount of processing. The level is then repeatable, and it means "dB of processing".

Setting up

  1. Set the SSB power to the peak power you want. With CESSB on, this is the PEP.
  2. Menu → Microphone → Enable CESSB. Set CESSB level (dB) to 0.
  3. Into a dummy load, talk normally. Adjust the mic gain until the C bar (below) just flickers on your loudest syllables, 0–2 dB. Your peaks now reach the set power.
  4. Raise the CESSB level to 4–6 dB. The C bar should now peak at about that amount on normal speech.
  5. Check on the air. If reports say you sound processed, step the level down.

Over CAT: PR1; enables CESSB, PL060050; sets 6 dB, and RM2; then RM; reads the meter.

7. Limitations and open items

  • Measured at RF on one radio, one band and one power. The KI3P prototype, 20 m, 5 W into a dummy load. Other bands, full power, other radios and on-air reports are still to do.
  • The PA sets the transmitted spectrum. On the KI3P prototype its two-tone IMD3 is −36 dB re PEP and it compresses about 1 dB near full power. CESSB adds nothing of its own at RF, but heavier processing makes the PA's products rise with the average power.
  • The ceiling assumes the default transmit equaliser at 800 Hz. The SSB power calibration runs through the equaliser. If you change the equaliser around 800 Hz after calibrating, the ceiling and the power setting drift apart by that amount.
  • The exciter's image floor on the KI3P prototype (−40 to −63 dBc, varying from one capture to the next) is under investigation. It is independent of CESSB.
  • Off by default. CESSB is opt-in, so existing users' on-air sound does not change until they enable it.
  • Still to do: user documentation (Phase 4), then closing #35.
